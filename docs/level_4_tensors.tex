\section{Level 4: Wigner--Eckart theorem, selection rules and line intensities}
Levels 1--3 gave the \emph{positions} of the energy levels. To predict a spectrum we also need
which transitions occur, how polarised they are and how bright. All three follow from one fact:
the electric-dipole operator $\vb{r}$ is a rank-1 spherical tensor.

\subsection{Matrix elements}
Write $\vb{r} = r\,\vb{C}^{(1)}$ with components $C^{(1)}_q$, $q\in\{-1,0,+1\}$. The
Wigner--Eckart theorem factorises every matrix element into a geometric part (a 3-$j$ symbol) and a
radial part that is the same for all $m$:
\begin{equation}
  \bra{l'm'} C^{(1)}_q \ket{l\,m}
  = (-1)^{m'}\sqrt{(2l+1)(2l'+1)}
    \begin{pmatrix} l' & 1 & l\\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}
    \begin{pmatrix} l' & 1 & l\\ -m' & q & m\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The 3-$j$ symbols vanish unless
\begin{equation}
  l' = l\pm1 \ (\text{parity}),\qquad m' - m = q \in\{-1,0,+1\}.
\end{equation}
These are the selection rules. Light emitted with $q=0$ is $\pi$-polarised and with $q=\pm1$ is
$\sigma^\pm$. The dipole operator does not act on spin, so $m_s$ is conserved at the level of the
uncoupled basis $\ket{m_l,m_s}$.

\subsection{Intensities between exact eigenstates}
Spin--orbit coupling mixes the $\ket{m_l,m_s}$ states, so the eigenstates $\ket{u}$ (upper
manifold) and $\ket{k}$ (lower manifold) are superpositions. The intensity of the component
$u\to k$ with polarisation $q$ is
\begin{equation}
  I_{uk}^{(q)} \;\propto\; \big|\bra{u}\, D_q \ket{k}\big|^2,
\end{equation}
where $D_q$ is the dipole matrix in the uncoupled basis, and the states are the numerically
diagonalised eigenvectors. Intensities are normalised so that every upper state emits a total of 1.
Because $\sum_{q,k}|\bra{u}D_q\ket{k}|^2$ is the same for every $p$ state, this is automatic for
$p\to s$ transitions (checked by the tests).

\subsection{Result: the sodium D lines}

$\zeta$ from the measured fine-structure splitting, the model predicts, with no further input,
the following weak-field pattern (shifts in units of $\mu_B B$):

\subsection{Fitting parameters back from data}
The same machinery runs in reverse. Given measured level energies at several fields,
\texttt{src/fitting.py} varies $\zeta$ (and optionally $g_s$) to minimise the sum of squared
residuals. On synthetic data with $1\ \si{\micro eV}$ noise the true $\zeta$ is recovered to about
$0.01\,\%$ starting from an initial value that is off by a factor of 2 (Notebook~4).
